Bannock is a flour-and-leavening bread that needs no oven, no proofing, and no dairy, which is why it has survived on fires for a couple of centuries. Baking powder does its lifting on contact with water and again with heat, so the clock starts when you pour. Mix it, cook it, eat it.

The water goes in slowly for a reason. Flour absorption swings with humidity and how packed the bag got, and a dough that turns sticky cannot be wound around a stick or flipped in a pan without tearing. Kneading is what turns it from crumbs into something that holds together over a fire. The oil is not optional either: it keeps the crust from going to leather and gets the calorie count up to where a slice is worth carrying.
