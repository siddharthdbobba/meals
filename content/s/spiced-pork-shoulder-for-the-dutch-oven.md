Braising is the reason a cheap, tough shoulder is worth three hours of fire tending. Low wet heat melts the connective tissue into gelatin, which is what makes the meat shred and what gives the liquid enough body to become gravy. A hot fast fire does the opposite: it squeezes the muscle and you get dry pork in thin beer.

One pot does the searing and the braising, so there is only ever one thing to wash. The sear matters more than it looks like it should, since the browned crust is most of the flavor in the finished liquid. The vinegar at the end is not seasoning so much as a correction; three hours of fat and sugar need something sharp or the whole plate tastes flat. This is car-camping food. The shoulder needs a cooler and there is no way around that.
