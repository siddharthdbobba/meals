steps:
  - "Slice the venison across the grain into six slabs, then pound each one flat between two sheets of plastic. Thin is the whole point."
  - "Cook the bacon partway in the dutch oven over the fire, four or five minutes. It should bend, not snap. Pull it and leave the fat behind."
  - "Season each slab, smear it with mustard, and lay on two bacon slices, a pickle spear, and a handful of onion slivers. Roll it up tight and pin it shut with a toothpick or a loop of butcher's twine."
  - "Dust the rolls in flour and brown them all over in the bacon fat. Work in batches so they sear instead of steam."
  - "Return everything to the pot, pour in the bouillon, and cover. Bank coals under and on the lid."
  - "Hold a slow bubble for about 90 minutes, turning the rolls once. Add a splash of water if the pot runs dry."
  - "Pull the rolls out, whisk the leftover flour into the liquid, and let it tighten into gravy. Remove the toothpicks before anyone bites down on one."

Venison is lean, which means the usual cuts go leathery the moment you cook them past rare. This recipe solves that twice over. Pounding breaks the muscle fibers before heat ever touches them, and a long wet braise finishes the job. That is why it works on the mystery cuts and the roasts nobody wanted, not just the backstrap.

The bacon is structural, not decorative. Venison has almost no internal fat, so the roll needs a fat source on the inside to baste the meat as it cooks. Half-cooking the bacon first matters: raw bacon in a 90-minute braise renders into something limp and pale. Everything happens in one pot, so the fond from browning ends up in the gravy.
