An insulated food jar is a slow cooker that runs on one boil. You put boiling water and dry food in it at breakfast, and by the time you stop walking the noodles have softened, the soy curls have rehydrated, and the whole thing is still hot enough to steam. Nothing needs to happen at lunch except opening a lid, which matters on a cold ridge where a stove is a fifteen-minute negotiation with the wind.

The preheat is the part people skip and the part that decides the outcome. Cold steel steals a large share of the heat you just paid fuel for. Miso goes in at the pour rather than being boiled separately because it dissolves fine in hot water and carries the salt the discarded seasoning packet would have. Fat is the other thing to protect: a teaspoon of sesame oil sits on top, holds heat, and keeps the broth from tasting thin.
