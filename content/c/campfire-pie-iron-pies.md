A pie iron is a small hinged skillet that clamps two slices of bread into a sealed pocket. The heat crimps the crusts together, the sugar on the outside caramelizes against the metal, and the filling steams in its own moisture. Nothing here needs a pot, water, or a pan to wash.

Butter and sugar on the iron faces are not garnish. They are what keeps the bread from welding to the metal and what turns a plain toasted sandwich into something with a crust. Keep filling away from the edges or it leaks, burns on the outside of the iron, and the pocket never seals. Coals cook these better than flames — flames blacken the bread before the middle is hot.
