steps:
  - "At home, cover the combs with milk in a sealed container and leave them in the fridge overnight. Drain and rinse them in the morning. Pack them in a hard container on ice."
  - "At camp, put the combs in a pot with a tablespoon of salt and about a liter of water. Set it over the fire or in a dutch oven with coals under it."
  - "Bring it to a bare simmer and hold it there. Rolling boil tears the combs apart. Give it an hour to an hour and a half, until a comb gives with no resistance when you press it."
  - "Lift them out and let them cool enough to handle. The outer skin slips off under your thumb, the way it does on a tongue. Peel every one. This is the tedious part and there is no shortcut."
  - "Return the peeled combs to the pot with a splash of the braise liquid and set it back over low coals for a few minutes to glaze and warm through. Salt to taste and eat them hot."

Cockscombs are mostly collagen, and collagen only becomes food if you hold it at a low simmer long enough. That is the whole recipe. The result is soft and mildly chicken-flavored — closer to a dumpling than to liver — and none of the organ-meat character people brace for.

The milk soak is a home step, not a camp step, because it needs a real fridge overnight. Everything after it is one pot and a fire. Size matters more than technique here: big combs peel in seconds and hold their shape, small ones cost you twenty minutes of picking at skin for very little in return. This is a first-night meal on a cooler trip, not something to carry into the backcountry.
